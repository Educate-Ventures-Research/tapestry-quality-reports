1. Purpose and scope

Tapestry already has 18 automated tests that check things like "did it cite a real source" and "did it stay in scope." Those are good tests, but they were written by guessing what might go wrong — nobody had actually sat down and read through what real people experienced with the old tool first.

So that's what we did here: read 136 real conversations from the old tool, wrote down anything that looked wrong, and used the repeated problems to shape the new test cases at the end of this document.

2. Where the data came from

"The old Tapestry" is a separate app called lm-chat — the tool people used before this one, with its own database. We read 136 real conversations from it (Oct 2025 – Sep 2026) directly from that database, look-only — nothing was changed, written back, or saved into any code repository. The raw conversations were kept on one machine, temporarily, just for this review.

The 4D AI Strategy Framework — a practical introduction EVR’s 4D AI Strategy Framework is a purpose-led approach to AI strategy: rather than beginning with a tool or a technology trend, it starts with the organisation’s most important educational or operational challenges and asks where AI can make a meaningful, safe and measurable contribution. The framework treats AI adoption as a whole-system change effort—not a standalone IT project. The four dimensions are interconnected. A weak link in any one of them—such as unclear governance, poor data foundations, insufficient staff confidence, or pilots that never translate into practice—can prevent an otherwise promising AI initiative from delivering value. 1. Governance and ethics This dimension establishes who is accountable, what is permitted, and how decisions will be made. It includes: A clear AI vision aligned to organisational or educational priorities Roles, responsibilities and decision-making structures Policies for safe, ethical and appropriate use Risk management for issues such as privacy, bias, safeguarding, equity, transparency and academic integrity Processes that balance innovation with oversight rather than treating them as opposites In education, for example, governance needs to address not only legal or technical compliance but also whether an AI use is pedagogically appropriate, inclusive and aligned with the institution’s values. EVR materials stress that ethical issues should be integral to the governance approach, including data privacy, bias, inclusion and language diversity. Question to ask: What rules, accountabilities and ethical safeguards enable us to innovate responsibly? 2. Technology and data This dimension concerns the foundations that make AI feasible, secure and equitable. It looks at: Technical infrastructure, connectivity and access Data quality, availability, ownership and management Privacy, cybersecurity and data-protection protocols Interoperability with existing platforms and workflows Vendor assessment and the practical costs of deployment and maintenance Whether data generated through AI use can support learning, evaluation and improvement The point is not to pursue sophisticated technology for its own sake. It is to ensure that the chosen technology and data arrangements are fit for the intended purpose—and do not deepen inequality of access. The framework also positions data strategically: AI-generated data can create useful insight into learning outcomes and inform planning and intervention at both institution and system levels. Question to ask: Do we have the data, infrastructure, security and access arrangements needed to use this AI well? 3. Staff capability and development AI strategy succeeds or fails through people. This dimension addresses the knowledge, confidence, skills and support needed for staff—and, in education, learners and families—to use AI effectively. It includes: Baseline AI literacy: what AI can and cannot do Role-specific capability, beyond generic training Pedagogical and professional judgement when working with AI Training in safe, ethical and critical use Change-management support and communities of practice Delivery models such as AI champions and train-the-trainer approaches Learner AI literacy and appropriate curriculum integration A crucial principle is that capability is not simply “training everyone to prompt.” It includes developing the capacity to evaluate outputs, recognise limitations, redesign workflows, protect learners, and make sound human decisions. EVR’s framework explicitly considers capacity-building through models such as AI champions, as well as AI literacy for learners and effective curriculum integration. Question to ask: Who needs to be able to do what differently—and what sustained support will make that possible? 4. Iterative unit applications This is where strategy becomes real: selecting, piloting, evaluating and refining AI use cases in particular teams, schools, departments or services. Rather than attempting institution-wide transformation all at once, this dimension encourages organisations to: Identify a high-priority challenge or opportunity. Choose a focused, feasible AI use case. Define intended beneficiaries, outcomes and risks. Pilot it in a relevant unit or context. Gather evidence about implementation, impact and unintended effects. Improve, adapt, scale—or stop—the initiative based on what is learned. The “iterative” part is vital. Pilot results should feed back into governance, technology/data decisions and staff-development plans, continually strengthening the wider strategy. Question to ask: Which specific use cases should we test first, how will we learn from them, and what evidence would justify scaling them? How the dimensions work together A useful way to think of the framework is: Dimension | Primary focus | Typical failure if neglected Governance & ethics | Purpose, accountability, safe boundaries | Uncontrolled or inconsistent AI use Technology & data | Infrastructure, access, security, data quality | Tools that cannot be implemented reliably or equitably Staff capability | Skills, confidence, professional practice | Low adoption, unsafe practice, superficial use Iterative unit applications | Practical pilots, evidence and scale | Strategy remains theoretical; pilots fail to inform change The framework is therefore not a checklist completed once. It is a continuous learning cycle, where progress in one dimension informs adjustments in the others. EVR describes effective strategy as ensuring both that every dimension is adequately addressed and that alignment between dimensions is deliberately strengthened. A simple example Imagine a school group wants to use AI to reduce teacher workload while improving feedback to students. Governance & ethics: Define acceptable uses; clarify staff accountability; establish rules for student data and checking AI-generated feedback. Technology & data: Assess whether the selected tools meet privacy, security, integration and access requirements. Staff capability: Train teachers not just to generate feedback, but to review it critically, adapt it pedagogically and explain appropriate AI use to students. Iterative unit applications: Trial the approach with a small group of teachers, assess workload, feedback quality, student experience and equity implications, then refine before wider rollout. The strategic test is not “Does the tool work?” It is: Does this use of AI advance our chosen outcomes, safely, fairly and sustainably? The core mindset The framework is guided by a coherent, context-specific vision for AI, grounded in real challenges and opportunities—not by a desire to deploy AI everywhere. EVR materials describe this as a purpose-driven approach, with educational outcomes taking priority over technology trends. A concise way to remember it: Start with purpose. Build the conditions.
