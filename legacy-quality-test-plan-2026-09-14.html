1. Purpose and scope

Tapestry already has 18 automated tests that check things like "did it cite a real source" and "did it stay in scope." Those are good tests, but they were written by guessing what might go wrong — nobody had actually sat down and read through what real people experienced with the old tool first.

So that's what we did here: read 136 real conversations from the old tool, wrote down anything that looked wrong, and used the repeated problems to shape the new test cases at the end of this document.

2. Where the data came from

"The old Tapestry" is a separate app called lm-chat — the tool people used before this one, with its own database. We read 136 real conversations from it (Oct 2025 – Sep 2026) directly from that database, look-only — nothing was changed, written back, or saved into any code repository. The raw conversations were kept on one machine, temporarily, just for this review.

Recommended approach: a small, paid discovery-to-decision engagement Do not lead with a full trust-wide strategy, audit, or CPD programme. With unclear goals and a limited budget, the immediate job is to help the trust define a priority problem worth solving—and give them a credible, affordable next step. 1. Offer a tightly scoped “AI/EdTech Priorities Sprint” Position this as a short, fixed-scope engagement rather than consultancy with an open-ended brief. Suggested components One leadership discovery session with the CEO/executive lead, digital/IT lead, safeguarding lead, school-improvement representation, and one or two headteachers. A short pre-session questionnaire to identify: current tools and use cases; greatest pressures or opportunities; policy/safeguarding concerns; staff confidence and capability; available budget, decision process, and timescale. A practical output: a concise 90-day priorities roadmap containing: two or three agreed priority outcomes; essential governance and safeguarding actions; a recommendation on whether to proceed with CPD, a pilot, an audit, or no further investment yet; an indicative phased costed pathway. This gives the trust something tangible for its board or executive team, while ensuring EVR is not doing extensive unpaid scoping. 2. Frame the discussion around educational and organisational problems—not AI tools Ask them to select from a limited set of trust-level questions, for example: Where are staff or leaders losing disproportionate time? What inconsistency across schools is causing risk or inequity? What teaching-and-learning challenge might AI or digital tools plausibly help address? What must be true for AI use to be safe, compliant, and educationally worthwhile? What would count as success within one term? The EVR 4D framework is useful here: use cases, technology/data, staff capability, and governance/ethics. It prevents the conversation being reduced to “which tool should we buy?” and makes clear that training alone may not be the right first investment. 3. Give them three genuinely modular options Avoid a large menu of sessions. Offer a clear choice after the sprint: Option Best when Outcome A. Foundations The main need is shared understanding and immediate risk reduction Leadership briefing plus a staff-facing essentials/safeguarding session B. Focused pilot They have one plausible priority but need evidence before scaling One use case, a small number of schools, logic model, light-touch evaluation C. Strategy and capability pathway They identify a trust-wide imperative and can commit funding Diagnostic, leadership strategy work, selected CPD, school-level implementation support Make clear that Option A or B is the likely starting point, not Option C. The trust should earn the right to scale by demonstrating a clear purpose, leadership ownership, and implementation capacity. 4. Keep a clear boundary between discovery and delivery A common failure mode is producing a broad proposal before the client can articulate the problem. Internal experience suggests that early-stage MAT conversations progress better when EVR uses structured discovery, concrete examples, and lightweight pilots to convert interest into a defined opportunity and budget. Therefore: charge for the sprint, even if modestly; specify attendees, preparation required, outputs, and timetable; do not promise a bespoke trust-wide strategy as part of the initial fee; make any subsequent work contingent on an agreed problem statement, sponsor, budget range, and decision date. 5. Use peer learning and internal capacity to make the budget go further For a cost-constrained trust, recommend: a small cross-trust working group rather than training every member of staff; a mixed group of operational, safeguarding, pedagogical, and school leaders—not only IT staff; train-the-trainer or champion capacity only after priorities are agreed; virtual delivery where it reduces cost and makes cross-school participation easier; existing resources and tools where possible, before proposing new licences. This mirrors EVR’s trust-wide model: develop direction centrally, then support schools to adapt it to their own contexts and use the resulting strategy to target CPD. A MAT-wide technology transformation example also highlights the value of central support combined with sustainable, targeted staff development rather than treating training as an optional extra. Suggested positioning for the follow-up It sounds as though the most useful next step is not a large AI programme, but a short piece of structured work to establish where AI could—or should not—add value across the trust. We would help a small leadership group identify the most pressing priorities, clarify essential safeguarding and governance considerations, and leave you with a practical 90-day roadmap and proportionate options for next steps. That would allow you to make a sound decision before committing scarce CPD or technology budget.
